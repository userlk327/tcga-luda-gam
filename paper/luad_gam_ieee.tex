\title{Tumor Stage Carries the Transportable Signal in Cross-Cohort Lung
Adenocarcinoma Survival Prediction: A Controlled Benchmark of Machine-Learning
and Additive Models}

\begin{abstract}
Machine-learning survival models for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) are usually
reported on a single cohort and rarely tested on independent data, so it is
seldom clear what actually transports to a new population. We benchmarked four
machine-learning survival models (random survival forest, gradient boosting
survival, XGBoost with a Cox objective, and DeepSurv), a spline
generalized-additive-model (GAM) stacking ensemble, and an interpretable
feature-level additive GAM, together with clinical and stage-only baselines,
using TCGA-LUAD ($n=501$; $181$ deaths) for development and an independent
East-Asian cohort (OncoSG, $n=302$; $94$ deaths) for external validation, on the
features shared by both cohorts. Three findings emerged, none of them the
headline we first expected. First, most of the transportable signal is
tumor stage: a stage-and-sex model reached external concordance $C=0.691$,
adding genomic features (tumor mutational burden, fraction of genome altered) to
an additive model did not improve it ($0.682$), and the best machine-learning
model ($0.705$) did not significantly exceed the stage-and-sex baseline under
paired bootstrap. Second, one genomic feature was
actively harmful: a raw mutation count, nearly collinear with mutational burden
(Spearman $0.98$) and measured $3.4\times$ lower in the external cohort,
significantly degraded XGBoost externally (paired bootstrap $+0.030$,
$95\%$~CI~$[0.009,0.052]$ on removal) but left additive and forest models
untouched---a model-specific, not complexity-driven, effect. Third, every
model's concordance rose from internal to external evaluation, which reweighting
to the development cohort's stage distribution shows to be substantially a
case-mix effect---OncoSG's steeper stage--risk gradient---rather than
transportability, cautioning against reading cross-cohort concordance as a
transfer metric. Differences between the best models were not statistically
significant under paired bootstrap. We conclude that in cross-cohort LUAD
prognosis a simple, interpretable clinical model is hard to beat, genomic
summary features transfer poorly and can mislead flexible learners, and
apparent model rankings must be read against cohort case-mix.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================
\IEEEPARstart{L}{ung} adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most common histological
subtype of lung cancer and a leading cause of cancer death worldwide~\cite{siegel2023}.
A large literature applies machine learning to LUAD survival prediction, usually
on a single cohort with internal cross-validation. Internal performance,
however, is a weak guide to behavior on a new population~\cite{tripod2015}: an
external cohort can differ in ancestry, in case mix, and in the sequencing
platform used to derive molecular features. The practical question is not which
model scores highest on the development data, but what actually transports---and
that question is rarely asked.

We set out to answer it with a controlled comparison. We trained four
machine-learning survival models and a stacking ensemble, contrasted them with a
transparent feature-level additive model, and---because we wanted to know what
each component was worth---compared all of them against clinical and stage-only
baselines. So that every fitted model could be transported without
modification, we confined them to the features recorded in both our development
cohort (TCGA-LUAD) and an independent validation cohort (OncoSG).

The results were more sobering, and more useful, than a leaderboard. Most
of the signal that survived the move to the external cohort was tumor stage; the
genomic features added little; the differences among the
better models were within statistical noise; and one redundant, platform-shifted
genomic feature quietly degraded a specific model. We also found that every
model appeared to \emph{improve} on the external cohort, which turned out to be
a property of that cohort's case mix rather than of the models. Taken together,
these observations argue for humility about what cross-cohort benchmarks can
show and for treating a simple clinical model as the bar any complex model must
clear.

Our contributions are: (i) a leakage-free benchmark of six modeling strategies
plus clinical baselines, evaluated internally and on a genuinely independent
cohort with paired-bootstrap inference; (ii) evidence that tumor stage carries
most of the transportable prognostic signal in this setting, that genomic summary
features transfer poorly, and that one such feature can significantly harm a
flexible model; and (iii) a case-mix analysis showing that cross-cohort
concordance can rise for reasons unrelated to transportability, so that model
rankings across cohorts must be read with care.

\section{Materials and Methods}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Cohorts}
The development cohort was the TCGA lung adenocarcinoma project
(\texttt{luad\_tcga\_pan\_can\_atlas\_2018})~\cite{tcga2014}, obtained through
cBioPortal~\cite{cbioportal2012}. After removing patients with missing or
non-positive overall-survival time, $501$ patients remained, of whom $181$
($36.1\%$) had a recorded death. The external validation cohort was the OncoSG
East-Asian lung adenocarcinoma study (\texttt{luad\_oncosg\_2020})~\cite{chen2020},
also from cBioPortal, with $302$ evaluable patients and $94$ deaths ($31.1\%$).
The cohorts differ in ancestry, geography, sequencing protocol, and---as we
quantify below---stage distribution, which is what makes the external test
demanding.

\subsection{Features, harmonization, and transportability}
Only features present in \emph{both} cohorts were used. Six were shared: age at
diagnosis, sex, AJCC tumor stage, tumor mutational burden (TMB, nonsynonymous),
somatic mutation count, and fraction of genome altered (FGA). Two facts shaped
the working set. TMB and mutation count are near-duplicates (Spearman
correlation $0.98$ in TCGA), so we retained TMB---the standardized
per-megabase metric---and dropped mutation count, leaving five features;
we report the effect of that removal explicitly, as it is central to one of our
findings. Both genomic metrics are also strongly platform-dependent: the external
cohort's median TMB and mutation count are about a third of TCGA's ($1.87$
vs.\ $6.68$; $56$ vs.\ $192$), reflecting the lower mutational burden of
predominantly non-smoking East-Asian LUAD and differences in sequencing. Because
the cohorts encode stage at different granularity, we collapsed both to the four
major stages (I--IV); sex and survival-status encodings were reconciled.

\subsection{Preprocessing}
Categorical variables (stage, sex) were one-hot encoded with the first level
dropped, and missing continuous values were median-imputed. For the additive
model, TMB was $\log(1+x)$-transformed and features were standardized, with
external spline inputs clipped to the training knot range so the basis was never
evaluated outside its support; tree learners are invariant to monotone
rescaling. All preprocessing was fit on the training data only, re-fit on each
training fold within the internal cross-validation, and fit on all of TCGA and
applied once to OncoSG for external validation. This strictly out-of-sample
scheme keeps the pipeline deployable for a single prospective patient---unlike
within-cohort standardization, which needs the entire test cohort to compute
local parameters and therefore cannot score a lone case.

\subsection{Models}
We compared six strategies and two baselines. The four base learners were a
random survival forest~\cite{ishwaran2008} ($500$ trees, $\sqrt{p}$ features per
split, minimum leaf size $3$); gradient boosting survival analysis with
regression-tree base learners ($500$ stages, learning rate $0.05$, depth $3$);
XGBoost~\cite{chen2016} with the \texttt{survival:cox} objective and early
stopping; and DeepSurv~\cite{katzman2018}, a Cox proportional-hazards neural
network ($128$--$64$--$1$ units, ReLU, dropout $0.3$, Adam with early stopping).
For \texttt{survival:cox} we encoded censoring through the sign of the label so
censored patients contribute to the risk sets. The fifth strategy was a stacking
ensemble~\cite{wolpert1992}: out-of-fold base predictions combined by a GAM
meta-learner. The sixth was a feature-level additive model~\cite{hastie1990}---an
elastic-net Cox proportional-hazards model~\cite{cox1972} ($\ell_1$ ratio $0.5$)
with cubic B-spline smooths (four degrees of freedom) on each continuous feature
and linear stage and sex terms. As baselines
we fit the same additive machinery on age$+$stage$+$sex (``clinical'') and on
stage$+$sex alone.

\subsection{Evaluation}
Discrimination was Harrell's concordance index ($C$)~\cite{harrell1996}.
Internal performance used leakage-free five-fold cross-validation with per-fold
preprocessing; the ensemble meta-learner was fit on inner out-of-fold
predictions. External performance used the model fit on all of TCGA and applied
once to OncoSG, with $95\%$ confidence intervals (CIs) from $1{,}000$ bootstrap
resamples. Crucially, because several of our claims concern \emph{differences}
between models or feature sets on the same patients, we quantified those with a
\emph{paired} bootstrap of the $C$-index difference (resampling patients and
recomputing both scores), which is far tighter than comparing marginal CIs. We
also stratified patients into risk tertiles (log-rank test, Kaplan--Meier
curves) and, for the additive model, tested nonlinearity with a
likelihood-ratio (LR) test against a linear Cox model, using the Cox partial
likelihood. To probe whether the internal-to-external difference reflected case
mix, we recomputed external $C$ with OncoSG stage-reweighted to TCGA's
distribution. A fixed random seed was used throughout; because DeepSurv is
sensitive to initialization, we report its external $C$ across five seeds.
Analyses used Python and scikit-survival~\cite{polsterl2020}.

\section{Results}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Everything clusters, and no model clearly wins externally}
Table~\ref{tab:cindex} reports discrimination for the six strategies on the five
transportable features. Internally the models spanned $C=0.626$ to $0.660$;
externally they spanned $0.636$ to $0.706$. XGBoost and DeepSurv were nominally
strongest externally ($0.705$, $0.706$), the additive GAM followed ($0.682$),
and the rest trailed---but a paired bootstrap of the differences found none of
them significant: XGBoost minus the additive GAM was $+0.024$
($95\%$~CI~$[-0.003,0.052]$) and DeepSurv minus the GAM was $+0.009$
($[-0.022,0.042]$). With $94$ external events the honest statement is that we
could not detect a difference between the leading models, not that one is best.
DeepSurv's external $C$ itself varied from $0.688$ to $0.720$ across five seeds
(mean $0.707$, SD $0.011$), comparable to the gaps between models.

\subsection{Tumor stage carries the transportable signal}
The comparison against baselines (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}) is the study's most
consequential result. A stage-and-sex additive model already reached external
$C=0.691$; adding age lifted it slightly to $0.695$; and adding the genomic
features (TMB and FGA) then \emph{lowered} it to $0.682$. The best
machine-learning model reached $0.705$---only $0.010$ above the clinical model
and $0.014$ above stage and sex alone. In other words, in this cross-cohort
setting most of the signal that transports is tumor stage; age contributes a
little; and the genomic summary features contribute nothing measurable. A paired
bootstrap confirms that none of these increments is significant: adding age gave
$+0.004$ ($95\%$~CI~$[-0.021,0.030]$) and adding the genomic features gave
$-0.014$ ($[-0.041,0.012]$). Crucially, the best machine-learning model did not
significantly exceed the stage-and-sex baseline either ($+0.014$,
$[-0.016,0.043]$). No addition to stage---clinical or genomic, simple or
complex---clears it by a detectable margin. These ladder comparisons, like the
removal tests, are unadjusted for multiplicity; since all cross zero, correction
would only reinforce the null.

\subsection{A non-transportable feature harms a specific model}
Genomic features not only fail to help; one can actively hurt. Table~\ref{tab:delta}
gives the paired-bootstrap effect of removing mutation count---the feature most
shifted across platforms. Its removal significantly improved XGBoost
($+0.030$, $95\%$~CI~$[0.009,0.052]$) and, within its seed variability,
marginally helped DeepSurv ($+0.022$, $[-0.007,0.049]$), but left the random
forest, gradient boosting, and the additive model statistically unchanged. The stacking ensemble showed the
largest shift of all (a $0.054$ point estimate), consistent with a meta-learner
compounding its base learners' corrupted predictions. Importantly, the two other
tree ensembles were unaffected, so this is \emph{not} a story about flexible
versus simple models: it is specific to XGBoost, DeepSurv, and the ensemble
built on them. This XGBoost effect is the only ML-related comparison to reach
significance; as one of roughly a dozen paired tests, with a lower confidence
bound ($0.009$) just above zero, it is best read as a nominal, boundary result
rather than a firmly established effect. Mechanistically, a learner that fit
fine structure in TCGA's
mutation-count distribution is misled when the external cohort's values sit far
below TCGA's; a penalized additive model with a clipped spline has little such
structure to lose, though shrinkage and clipping---not the additive form
alone---plausibly share the credit.

\subsection{Rising external concordance is a case-mix effect}
Every model's $C$ rose from internal to external evaluation
(Table~\ref{tab:cindex}), which is unusual---models normally degrade out of
sample. The explanation is, at least in part, case mix, and we can show it
directly. OncoSG contains far more advanced-stage disease than TCGA (stage
I/II/III/IV: $45/18/30/6\%$ vs.\ $54/24/16/5\%$) and has a steeper stage--risk
gradient (stage-IV event rate $0.74$ vs.\ $0.62$; stages I--III lower than
TCGA). A cohort in which stage separates risk more sharply is easier to
discriminate. When we reweight OncoSG to TCGA's stage distribution, external
concordance falls by $0.014$--$0.020$ (the additive model from $0.682$ to
$0.668$, XGBoost from $0.705$ to $0.689$), recovering a substantial part of the
internal-to-external rise---though not all of it, so case mix contributes to but
does not fully explain the inflation. This is a caution for the field: a higher $C$ on an external
cohort does not by itself demonstrate good transfer.

\subsection{Risk stratification and interpretability}
The additive model produced clean external risk groups: tertiles of its risk
score separated OncoSG survival with a log-rank $p=2.2\times10^{-6}$ between low-
and high-risk groups (Fig.~\ref{fig:km}; multivariable $p=9.0\times10^{-6}$),
and $p=1.5\times10^{-9}$ on TCGA. Its smooths (Fig.~\ref{fig:smooths}) and the LR
test (Table~\ref{tab:lr}) show that nonlinearity is, at most, weakly supported:
the overall test is marginal ($\text{LR}=13.7$, $\mathrm{df}=6$, $p=0.033$) and,
of the three continuous features tested, only age reaches nominal significance
($p=0.032$)---which does not survive a Bonferroni correction for three tests
($0.017$). The honest reading is that nonlinearity is barely detectable and, if
present, is an age effect (a shallow J-shape, whose steep tails sit in sparse
data); TMB and FGA are adequately linear. The value of the additive model here
is transparency and robustness rather than a nonlinear edge.

\subsection{Robustness of the preprocessing choice}
Because the genomic features are platform-shifted, one might standardize the
external features within OncoSG rather than with TCGA parameters. We deliberately
did not, since within-cohort standardization needs the whole test cohort and so
cannot score a lone prospective patient. Empirically it also does not help: a
sensitivity analysis found it changed external $C$ by at most $0.026$, with mixed
sign, slightly reducing the two strongest models. The results are not an artifact
of the standardization scheme.

\section{Discussion}
% =====================================================================
Read together, our results tell a modest but useful story. In cross-cohort LUAD
survival prediction, the signal that transports is dominated by tumor stage; a
clinical (age, stage, sex) model is within $0.01$ of the best of six
machine-learning strategies, a gap that is not statistically significant under
paired bootstrap, and adding genomic summary features does not improve---and if
anything worsens---external discrimination. The differences among the leading
models are not statistically significant at this sample size, and the one place
a genomic feature clearly mattered, it \emph{hurt}: a redundant, platform-shifted
mutation count significantly degraded XGBoost while leaving forests and the
additive model untouched. That the harm was confined to particular models, not
to a model class, is itself instructive---it is a feature-transportability
effect filtered through each model's willingness to exploit fine structure, an
instance of the sensitivity of learned models to dataset shift~\cite{moreno2012},
rather than a simple lesson about complexity.

The internal-to-external rise in concordance is a reminder that cross-cohort
$C$-indices are not a clean transfer metric. Because OncoSG's case mix makes
stage a sharper discriminator, every model scored higher there; a naive reading
would credit the models with excellent transfer, when much of the effect belongs
to the cohort. Benchmarks that compare models across cohorts should report case
mix and, where possible, hold it constant.

None of this argues against interpretable modeling---if anything it argues for it.
An additive model matched the best learners within statistical noise, stratified
the external cohort as sharply as any model, and was the model least disturbed by
the harmful feature; its transparency also makes it a natural reference against
which to detect when a flexible model is exploiting non-transportable structure.
We caution, though, against over-crediting its ``additive form'': elastic-net
shrinkage and spline clipping are equally plausible sources of its robustness.

Our study has clear limitations. It uses a single external cohort, and the
finding that stage dominates may itself be specific to this cohort pair; a
second cohort would test it. Because the thesis rests on stage, it also assumes
the two cohorts stage patients comparably: if they use different AJCC editions
or pathological versus clinical staging, part of the apparent transportable
stage signal could be a harmonization artifact, which documented, matched
staging conventions would help resolve. Conversely, a convention mismatch would
tend to inject noise into stage and depress its transported signal, so the fact
that stage transports well is itself mild evidence that the conventions are not
badly discordant. The models are confined to six routinely available
features, and richer molecular data (gene expression, driver-gene status) might
transport differently. With $181$ development and $94$ external events the study
is underpowered to separate closely matched models, which is why we report
paired intervals and decline to name a winner. DeepSurv's seed sensitivity
(SD $0.011$) places its exact ranking within the noise floor. Finally, the
platform-shift analysis is observational: we show mutation count is
non-transportable and quantify its effect, but separating biological from
technical contributions to the shift would require harmonized re-measurement.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================
Benchmarking four machine-learning survival models, a stacking ensemble, an
interpretable additive model, and clinical baselines for LUAD under external
validation, we found that tumor stage carries most of the transportable
prognostic signal, that genomic summary features add little externally and one
can actively harm a flexible model, and that apparent gains in cross-cohort
concordance can reflect case mix rather than transfer. A simple, interpretable
clinical model is hard to beat in this setting and should be the baseline any
complex model must clear. The broader message is one of measurement discipline:
external validation is necessary but not sufficient, and its concordance must be
read against baselines, paired uncertainty, and cohort case mix before it is
taken as evidence that a model---or a feature---has generalized.

\section*{Reproducibility}
% =====================================================================
All preprocessing was fit on the development data only, with per-fold refitting
inside the internal cross-validation; a fixed random seed was used throughout,
