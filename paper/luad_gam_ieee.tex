\title{Feature Transportability, Not Model Complexity, Governs External
Generalization: A Controlled Survival-Model Benchmark in Lung Adenocarcinoma}

\begin{abstract}
Survival-prediction models for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) are usually reported
on a single cohort and rarely tested on independent data, so it is seldom clear
how they behave under the cohort and platform shifts they will meet in practice.
We set out to compare four machine-learning survival models (random survival
forest, gradient boosting survival, XGBoost with a Cox objective, and DeepSurv),
a spline generalized-additive-model (GAM) stacking ensemble, and a single,
interpretable feature-level additive GAM, using TCGA-LUAD ($n=501$; $181$
deaths) for development and an independent East-Asian cohort (OncoSG, $n=302$;
$94$ deaths) for external validation. All models were confined to the features
shared by both cohorts. Our central finding was unexpected. When we included a
mutation-count feature that is nearly collinear with tumor mutational burden
(Spearman $0.98$) and is measured $3.4\times$ lower in the external cohort, it
silently and substantially degraded the external generalization of the flexible
models---most severely the stacking ensemble, whose external concordance fell to
$0.595$---while leaving the additive GAM untouched. Removing this single feature
raised the ensemble to $0.649$ and lifted XGBoost and DeepSurv from $0.675$ and
$0.674$ to $0.705$ and $0.706$, without changing the additive model ($0.679$ to
$0.682$). On the cleaned feature set, modern machine learning generalized best,
and the interpretable additive GAM remained statistically comparable ($C=0.682$,
overlapping confidence intervals) while separating external risk tertiles
(log-rank $p=2.2\times10^{-6}$) and admitting a likelihood-ratio-tested
nonlinear age effect ($p=0.032$). We conclude that in cross-cohort survival
prediction, feature transportability can matter more than model choice: a single
non-transportable feature can dominate the apparent ranking of models, and an
interpretable additive model offers useful robustness insurance against it.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Survival analysis, lung adenocarcinoma, external validation, dataset shift,

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================
\IEEEPARstart{L}{ung} adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most common histological
subtype of lung cancer and a leading cause of cancer death worldwide~\cite{siegel2023}.
A large literature applies machine learning to LUAD survival prediction, yet
most models are reported on a single cohort and validated only internally.
Internal performance is a weak guide to how a model behaves on a new
population~\cite{tripod2015}, and the gap is not merely one of sample: an
external cohort can differ in ancestry, in the treatments patients received, and
in the sequencing platform used to derive molecular features. Whether a model
survives those shifts is the question that decides its clinical usefulness, and
it is rarely asked.

We began with a conventional comparison. We trained four machine-learning
survival models and a stacking ensemble that combines them, and we contrasted
them with a deliberately simple, interpretable alternative---a feature-level
generalized additive model (GAM) that keeps the Cox proportional-hazards
form~\cite{cox1972} but allows smooth nonlinear effects for continuous
covariates~\cite{hastie1990}. So that each fitted model could be transported
without modification, we restricted every model to the features recorded in both
our development cohort (TCGA-LUAD) and an independent validation cohort (OncoSG).

What we found was not the story we expected. In our first pass the stacking
ensemble, competitive on internal data, appeared to collapse on external
validation, and we were tempted to conclude that model complexity had failed to
generalize. On closer inspection the cause was much more specific. One of the
molecular features---a raw somatic mutation count---is almost perfectly
correlated with tumor mutational burden (TMB) and, because the two cohorts were
sequenced differently, is calibrated on a completely different scale in the
external data. That single non-transportable feature, not the model class, was
responsible for the apparent failure. Removing it reversed the ranking: the
flexible machine-learning models became the strongest external performers, and
the ensemble no longer collapsed.

This paper reports that investigation and its lesson. Our contributions are:
(i) a leakage-free protocol that benchmarks six modeling strategies on an
identical, transportable feature set, internally and on a genuinely independent
cohort; (ii) a controlled demonstration that a single redundant,
platform-shifted feature can silently dominate external generalization---severely
for flexible learners, negligibly for an additive model---and can invert the
conclusion one would draw about model complexity; and (iii) evidence that an
interpretable additive GAM, though not the top performer once features are
cleaned, is statistically comparable to the best model, robust to the offending
feature, and equipped with a likelihood-ratio test that keeps its nonlinearity
claims honest.

\section{Materials and Methods}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Cohorts}
The development cohort was the TCGA lung adenocarcinoma project
(\texttt{luad\_tcga\_pan\_can\_atlas\_2018})~\cite{tcga2014}, obtained through
cBioPortal~\cite{cbioportal2012}. After removing patients with missing or
non-positive overall-survival time, $501$ patients remained, of whom $181$
($36.1\%$) had a recorded death. The external validation cohort was the OncoSG
East-Asian lung adenocarcinoma study (\texttt{luad\_oncosg\_2020})~\cite{chen2020},
also from cBioPortal, with $302$ evaluable patients and $94$ deaths ($31.1\%$).
The cohorts differ in ancestry, geography, and sequencing protocol, which is
exactly what makes the external test informative.

\subsection{Features, harmonization, and a note on transportability}
Only features present in \emph{both} cohorts were used, so that the fitted model
could be applied unchanged to the external data. Six candidate features were
shared: age at diagnosis, sex, AJCC tumor stage, TMB (nonsynonymous), somatic
mutation count, and fraction of genome altered (FGA). Two observations shaped
the final set. First, TMB and mutation count are near-duplicates---their
Spearman correlation in TCGA is $0.98$---so including both is redundant and makes
their individual contributions uninterpretable. Second, both are strongly
platform-dependent: the external cohort's median TMB and mutation count are
about a third of TCGA's ($1.87$ vs.\ $6.68$; $56$ vs.\ $192$), reflecting the
lower mutational burden of predominantly non-smoking East-Asian LUAD and
differences in sequencing. We therefore retained TMB, the standardized
per-megabase metric, and dropped mutation count, leaving five features. As we
show below, that decision---made on principle---turned out to change the study's
conclusion, which is why we also report the six-feature results explicitly.
Because the cohorts encode stage at different granularity, we collapsed both to
the four major stages (I--IV); sex and survival-status encodings were reconciled
between cohorts.

\subsection{Preprocessing}
Categorical variables (stage, sex) were one-hot encoded with the first level
dropped, and missing continuous values were median-imputed. For the additive
model, TMB was $\log(1+x)$-transformed to tame its right skew, features were
standardized, and external spline inputs were clipped to the training knot range
so the basis was never evaluated outside its support; the tree-based learners
are invariant to monotone rescaling. All preprocessing was fit on the training
data only. Within the internal cross-validation it was re-fit on each training
fold and applied to the held-out fold, so that no information from held-out
patients entered model fitting; for external validation it was fit on all of
TCGA and applied once to OncoSG.

\subsection{Models}
We compared six strategies. The four base learners were: a random survival
forest~\cite{ishwaran2008} ($500$ trees, $\sqrt{p}$ features per split, minimum
leaf size $3$); gradient boosting survival analysis with regression-tree base
learners ($500$ stages, learning rate $0.05$, tree depth $3$); XGBoost~\cite{chen2016}
with the \texttt{survival:cox} objective and early stopping; and
DeepSurv~\cite{katzman2018}, a Cox proportional-hazards neural network
($128$--$64$--$1$ units, ReLU activations, dropout $0.3$, Adam optimization with
early stopping). For \texttt{survival:cox} we encoded censoring through the sign
of the label so that censored patients contribute to the risk sets rather than
being discarded.

The fifth strategy was a \emph{stacking ensemble}~\cite{wolpert1992}: out-of-fold predictions from
the four base learners were combined by a GAM meta-learner (an elastic-net Cox
model with cubic B-spline smooths on each base-learner score). Base-learner
early stopping within each fold used a nested internal split, never the held-out
fold on which predictions were formed.

The sixth strategy---the interpretable comparator---was a \emph{feature-level
additive survival model}: an elastic-net Cox model ($\ell_1$ ratio $0.5$) in
which each continuous feature enters through a cubic B-spline basis (four degrees
of freedom) and stage and sex enter linearly,
\begin{equation}
\log h_i(t) = \log h_0(t) + \sum_{k} f_k(x_{ik}) + \boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\mathbf{z}_i,
\end{equation}
where the $f_k$ are the estimated smooths for age, TMB, and FGA and
$\mathbf{z}_i$ collects the categorical terms. The regularization strength was
chosen by internal cross-validation. It is the only model whose effect for each
covariate can be read directly from a plot.

\subsection{Evaluation}
Discrimination was measured by Harrell's concordance index
($C$)~\cite{harrell1996}. Internal performance on TCGA used leakage-free
five-fold cross-validation with per-fold preprocessing; for the stacking
ensemble, the meta-learner was fit on inner out-of-fold predictions so that no
patient informed its own score. External performance used the model fit on all
of TCGA and applied once to OncoSG, with $95\%$ confidence intervals (CIs) from
$1{,}000$ bootstrap resamples of the external cohort. We stratified patients
into risk tertiles and compared survival with the log-rank test and
Kaplan--Meier curves. To test whether the additive model's flexibility was
warranted, we performed a likelihood-ratio (LR) test comparing the full spline
model against one in which the same continuous covariates entered linearly,
using the Cox partial likelihood, reporting an overall and a per-feature test.
All experiments used a fixed random seed and were implemented in Python with
scikit-survival~\cite{polsterl2020}.

\section{Results}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{On the cleaned feature set, machine learning generalizes best}
Table~\ref{tab:cindex} reports discrimination for all six models on the five
transportable features, internally and externally. Internally the models were
closely bunched ($C$ from $0.626$ to $0.660$), with the additive model best. The
external picture differs: XGBoost and DeepSurv are the strongest transporters
($C=0.705$ and $0.706$), each improving markedly over its internal estimate. The
additive GAM follows at $0.682$, statistically indistinguishable from the leaders
(its CI, $[0.626,0.740]$, overlaps theirs), and the stacking ensemble and the two
weaker trees trail. No model collapses; every model except gradient boosting
carries or improves its performance from TCGA to OncoSG. Read on its own, this
table says simply that flexible machine learning generalizes at least as well as
a simple additive model here---the opposite of what our first, six-feature
analysis suggested.

\subsection{A single non-transportable feature dominates the ranking}
The contrast that motivates this paper appears in Table~\ref{tab:delta}, which
gives external discrimination \emph{before and after} removing mutation count.
Adding this one redundant, platform-shifted feature does not perturb every model
equally. It costs the stacking ensemble $0.054$ of external $C$, and it masks
XGBoost and DeepSurv by about $0.03$ each---enough, in the six-feature analysis,
to drag the ensemble to worst place and to make the additive model look like the
best generalizer. The additive GAM, by contrast, is essentially unmoved
($+0.003$), and the two other tree ensembles change negligibly. In other words,
the feature the flexible learners could not transport was doing most of the work
in the apparent model ranking. Mechanistically this is unsurprising: a learner
that has fit structure in TCGA's mutation-count distribution is misled when the
external cohort's values sit almost entirely below TCGA's, whereas an additive
model with a mild penalty and a clipped, monotone-ish TMB spline barely reacts.

\subsection{Risk stratification holds in the external cohort}
Stratifying OncoSG patients into tertiles of the additive model's risk score
produced well-separated survival curves (Fig.~\ref{fig:km}), with a log-rank
$p=2.2\times10^{-6}$ between the low- and high-risk groups (multivariable
log-rank $p=9.0\times10^{-6}$); the same stratification on TCGA gave
$p=1.5\times10^{-9}$. The stacking ensemble also stratified the external cohort,
though more weakly ($p=2.8\times10^{-4}$), consistent with its lower
discrimination. Clean risk groups are therefore achievable externally with a
transparent model.

\subsection{Where nonlinearity is, and is not, justified}
Because the additive model's appeal is interpretability, we tested whether its
nonlinearity is real rather than decorative. The smooths are shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:smooths}, and the LR test in Table~\ref{tab:lr}. Allowing
nonlinearity improves fit over a linear Cox model overall ($\text{LR}=13.7$,
$\mathrm{df}=6$, $p=0.033$), and the improvement is carried almost entirely by
age ($p=0.032$), whose effect is J-shaped: risk falls through middle age and
rises in older patients. We attribute the significant nonlinearity to this
mid-range curvature, which sits in dense data; the steep behavior at the extreme
ages, where patients are sparse, should not be over-read. The TMB and FGA
smooths are \emph{not} statistically supported ($p=0.21$ and $p=0.27$), so for
those features a linear term suffices---a distinction the test exists to enforce.

\subsection{The additive result does not depend on the shifted genomic features}
To check that the additive model is not quietly relying on the platform-shifted
genomic features, we refit it on only age, stage, FGA, and sex---dropping TMB as
well as mutation count. External discrimination was essentially unchanged
($C=0.687$; internal $0.662$), and risk stratification remained strong
($p=3.0\times10^{-8}$). The additive model's transportability thus rests on the
well-matched clinical features, not on the genomic quantities that shift across
platforms.

\section{Discussion}
% =====================================================================
The lesson of this study is that in cross-cohort survival prediction, which
features one includes can matter more than which model one chooses. A single
feature---redundant with another and calibrated differently across sequencing
platforms---was enough to invert the ranking of six models: with it, an
interpretable additive model looked like the best generalizer and a stacking
ensemble looked like a failure; without it, flexible machine learning was clearly
strongest and the ensemble was unremarkable. The models differed only in how much
they leaned on the offending feature. Flexible learners, which can carve fine
structure out of the development distribution, were the most exposed when that
structure did not transport---an instance of the well-known sensitivity of
learned models to dataset shift~\cite{moreno2012}. The additive model, penalized
and constrained to a smooth per-feature form, had little structure to lose.

This reframes a tempting but wrong conclusion. Had we stopped at the six-feature
analysis, we would have reported that ensembling ``overfits'' and that simplicity
generalizes better. That reading does not survive a careful look: the ensemble
does not collapse once the non-transportable feature is removed, and the simple
model wins only by being unaffected by a variable that should not have been there
in the first place. The honest account is about feature transportability, and it
cuts against reflexively blaming or crediting model complexity.

None of this makes the interpretable model dispensable. On the cleaned features
it is statistically comparable to the best learners ($0.682$ vs.\ $0.706$, with
overlapping CIs), it stratifies the external cohort as sharply as any model, its
single nonlinear effect is backed by a formal test, and---most relevant
here---it was the model least disturbed by the feature that fooled the others.
For a prognostic tool that must travel between institutions, that robustness and
transparency are worth having even at a small cost in peak discrimination; at the
least, an additive model makes a useful reference against which to detect when a
flexible model is exploiting non-transportable structure.

Our study has clear limitations. It uses a single external cohort; a second would
strengthen the generalizability claim, though the feature set that transfers to
other cohorts may differ. The models are confined to six routinely available
clinical and genomic features, and richer molecular data (gene expression,
driver-gene status, or the TCGA-only scores we could not transport) may add
signal at the cost of transportability---the very tension this paper examines.
The event count ($181$ deaths) limits power, visible in the wide confidence
intervals. Finally, our platform-shift analysis is observational: we identify
mutation count as non-transportable and show its outsized effect, but a
prospective, harmonized re-measurement would be needed to separate biological
from technical contributions to the shift.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================
Comparing four machine-learning survival models, a stacking ensemble, and an
interpretable additive model for LUAD under external validation, we found that a
single redundant, platform-shifted feature dominated the apparent ranking:
including it made the flexible models look worse and the simple model look best,
while removing it reversed the order. On the cleaned feature set, machine
learning generalized best and the interpretable additive model remained
statistically comparable, robust, and transparent. The practical message is to
scrutinize feature transportability before attributing external success or
failure to model complexity, and to keep an interpretable model in the
comparison as a check on what the flexible ones are learning.
